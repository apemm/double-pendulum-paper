\title{How long is a simulated double pendulum right?\\
Step size, floating-point precision, and the Lyapunov exponent}

\ifanon

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Students who simulate a chaotic system learn that nearby initial conditions
separate exponentially, but they seldom ask how long the simulation itself,
which is perturbed at every step by truncation and by roundoff, remains
accurate. We describe a computational exercise that answers this question in
about forty lines of code. We integrate the double pendulum with a fixed-step
fourth-order Runge--Kutta method in arbitrary-precision arithmetic, so that the
step $h$ and the number of significand bits $p$ can be varied independently, and
we measure the time $T$ for which each computed trajectory agrees with a far
more accurate reference. The results follow a single formula in which the only
property of the pendulum is the largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$. Until the
other source of error takes over, halving $h$ extends $T$ by $4\ln 2/\lambda$
and each additional bit extends it by $\ln 2/\lambda$, so the numerical error
measures $\lambda$. The two values obtained this way,
$\lamStep\pm\lamStepSE$ and $\lamPrec\pm\lamPrecSE$ in natural units, match the
growth rate of a deliberately perturbed trajectory over the same interval and
lie within 20\% of the long-time exponent, \lamBen. In double precision, no
choice of step keeps this pendulum accurate beyond about \TBestDouble\ natural
time units, roughly \TBestDoubleSec~s for rods one meter long, and the
computational work needed grows exponentially with $T$. Meanwhile, energy
remains constant to $10^{-\dEexp}$ in units of $mgl$ long after the trajectory
has become wrong.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The double pendulum is the standard classroom example of chaos in a mechanical
system, and the teaching journals have treated it as one for thirty years.
Shinbrot \textit{et al.}\cite{shinbrot1992} released two nearly identical
physical pendulums together and measured an exponential growth rate of
$7.5\pm1.5~\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ for their separation. Levien and
Tan\cite{levien1993} built a double pendulum for the undergraduate laboratory
and compared its motion with a numerical model. More recently,
D'Alessio\cite{dalessio2023} combined analysis, simulation, and experiment, and
computed the full spectrum of Lyapunov exponents. All three papers ask how the
\emph{physical} system amplifies an uncertainty in its initial state. Rafat
\textit{et al.}\cite{rafat2009} studied a compound variant with square plates
in place of the point masses, and Calv\~ao and Penna\cite{calvao2015} studied
the point-mass system numerically.

A simulation amplifies errors in the same way, but its errors are introduced at
every step rather than only in the initial condition. These errors are small and
come from truncating the Taylor series of the solution and from rounding each
arithmetic result to a finite number of bits, and each error is amplified from
the step at which it is introduced. Hence, a student who animates a double
pendulum for a minute cannot tell whether the last forty seconds still follow
the solution of the equations that were typed in. The checks that students
usually apply do not answer this question, since a trajectory that is entirely
wrong can still conserve energy to many digits, as we show in
Sec.~\ref{sec:energy}. Likewise, halving the step and seeing no visible change
only tests the interval over which both runs are still accurate.

We describe an exercise that answers this question quantitatively, but none of
the underlying facts is new. Lorenz\cite{lorenz1963} showed that a simple
deterministic system can depend sensitively on its initial condition, and
Lighthill\cite{lighthill1986} gave the horizon of predictability its name. The
competition between truncation and roundoff is standard numerical
analysis,\cite{hairer1993} and the reliability of computed chaotic solutions has
been studied with multiple-precision arithmetic\cite{liao2009} and in
atmospheric models, where Teixeira \textit{et al.}\cite{teixeira2007} found that
the time at which runs with different steps decouple is logarithmic in the
step. In particular, Li \textit{et al.}\cite{li2001} showed that a given
precision has a best step and a longest reliable time, and Kehlet and
Logg\cite{kehlet2017} derived an error estimate of the form we use below. Wang
and Li\cite{wang2014} found that the reliable time is linear in the number of
digits, with a slope set by the Lyapunov exponent, and Mendes and
Nepomuceno\cite{mendes2016} estimated the exponent from the divergence of two
equivalent computations that round differently. These studies treat the Lorenz
and similar systems and are written for researchers. In this journal,
Hayes\cite{hayes2004} discussed what a computed chaotic trajectory means, and
Faux and Godolphin\cite{faux2021} collected floating-point pitfalls for
students. In an undergraduate thesis, Wild\cite{wild2019} integrated the double
pendulum with RK4 at the IEEE significand widths and observed that the wider
formats follow a 113-bit solution for longer, but did not relate this time to
the Lyapunov exponent. However, we have not found a treatment at the level of
an undergraduate course in which the student separates the two sources of
error, measures each, and connects both to the Lyapunov exponent, so we give
such a treatment here.
\ifdefined\longversion
The exercise teaches five things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The time for which a simulation of a chaotic system is accurate depends
only logarithmically on the error introduced per step, so large improvements in
the integrator only add a small amount to that time.
\item Truncation error and roundoff error can be isolated from one another once
the precision of the arithmetic is a parameter of the program rather than a
property of the hardware.
\item Numerical error is itself a small perturbation, so its growth measures the
Lyapunov exponent as well as a deliberately perturbed initial condition does.
\item For a given floating-point format there is a best step, and a longest
accurate time that no step can improve upon.
\item Conservation of energy is not evidence that a chaotic trajectory is
correct.
\end{enumerate}
\else

First, the exercise teaches that the time for which a simulation of a chaotic
system is accurate depends only logarithmically on the error introduced per
step. Second, truncation and roundoff can be isolated once the precision of the
arithmetic is a parameter of the program. Third, numerical error is a small

\fi

\section{Model and numerical method}

\subsection{Equations of motion}

Two point masses $m$ hang in series from a fixed pivot on massless rigid rods of
length $l$ and move in a vertical plane under gravity. We measure the angles
$\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ from the downward vertical and time in units of
$\sqrt{l/g}$, so the Lagrangian equations of motion contain no parameters:
\begin{align}
\ddot\theta_1 &= \frac{\omega_1^2\sin\Delta\cos\Delta+\sin\theta_2\cos\Delta
                +\omega_2^2\sin\Delta-2\sin\theta_1}{2-\cos^2\Delta},\label{eq:eom1}\\
\ddot\theta_2 &= \frac{-\omega_2^2\sin\Delta\cos\Delta
                +2\left(\sin\theta_1\cos\Delta-\omega_1^2\sin\Delta-\sin\theta_2\right)}
                {2-\cos^2\Delta},\label{eq:eom2}
\end{align}
where $\omega_i=\dot\theta_i$ and $\Delta=\theta_2-\theta_1$. The energy, in
units of $mgl$, is
\begin{equation}
E=\omega_1^2+\tfrac12\omega_2^2+\omega_1\omega_2\cos\Delta-2\cos\theta_1-\cos\theta_2 .
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
Every run starts from rest with both rods horizontal,
$\theta_1=\theta_2=\pi/2$, so $E=0$. One natural time unit is \secPerUnit~s when
$l=1$~m.

\subsection{Integrator and arithmetic}

We write Eqs.~(\ref{eq:eom1}) and (\ref{eq:eom2}) as a first-order system
$\dot{\bm y}=\bm f(\bm y)$ for $\bm y=(\theta_1,\theta_2,\omega_1,\omega_2)$ and
advance it with the classical fourth-order Runge--Kutta method (RK4) at a fixed
step $h$. The exercise requires a fixed step, since an adaptive integrator
adjusts $h$ to hold its estimate of the local error near a tolerance, which
hides the dependence we want to analyze.

All arithmetic uses the MPFR library,\cite{fousse2007} which rounds every
operation correctly to a significand of $p$ bits, where the program chooses
$p$. We call MPFR from Python through the \texttt{gmpy2} package, and Julia's
built-in \texttt{BigFloat} type wraps the same library and works equally well.
The values $p=24$, 53, 64, and 113 reproduce the significand widths of IEEE
single, double, extended, and quadruple precision.\cite{ieee754,goldberg1991} The
256-bit format has $p=237$, which we treat as exact arithmetic, since its
rounding unit is $2^{-237}\approx10^{-71}$.

We choose steps $h=2^{-k}$ because a power of two is represented exactly at
every precision, so lowering $p$ changes only the arithmetic. A decimal step
such as $10^{-4}$ would be rounded differently at each precision, so runs
intended to differ only in their arithmetic would also use slightly different
steps. Wild\cite{wild2019} observed the effect of this choice, since replacing
$h=10^{-4}$ by $h=2^{-13}$ kept the 53-bit and 64-bit solutions close to the
113-bit solution for longer.

\subsection{Reference solution and measure of error}

We measure the error of a run by the distance $d(t)$ between its lower bob and
that of a reference run, in units of $l$. Since both bobs lie within $2l$ of the
pivot, $d$ cannot exceed 4, and its running maximum levels off near that bound
once two runs are unrelated. We define the
\emph{horizon} $T$ of a run as the first time at which $d\ge10^{-2}$, which is
roughly when two superimposed animations become visibly different.

No exact solution is available, so the reference is a much more accurate run,
and we show how far it can be trusted. Let $e_a$ and $e_r$ be the true errors of
a run and the reference. By the triangle inequality, the measured separation
satisfies $|d-e_a|\le e_r$. All errors are amplified at the same rate, so the
ratio $e_r/e_a$ stays close to its initial value, which for two RK4 runs with
steps $2^{-k_r}$ and $2^{-k_a}$ is $2^{-4(k_r-k_a)}$. Hence, against a reference
with $k_r=18$, the separation gives the true error of a run with $k_a\le16$ to
better than one part in 250, as long as the run itself is accurate.

\subsection{Three experiments}

\emph{Truncation alone.} With $p=237$, we vary $k$ from 4 to 17 and compare each
run with the reference $k=18$. Roundoff is about fifty orders of magnitude below
the truncation error and plays no part.

\emph{Roundoff alone.} With $h$ fixed, we vary $p$ from 11 to 128 and compare
each run with the run at $p=237$ \emph{and the same} $h$. Both runs iterate the
same discrete map, so the truncation error is common to them and cancels
exactly, which leaves only the effect of rounding. We use a multiple-precision
library because hardware arithmetic does not allow this separation.

\emph{Both together.} With $p=24$, 32, 40, 53, and 64, we vary $k$ and compare
with the reference of the first experiment. This is the situation of a student
working in ordinary single or double precision.

\section{The horizon of predictability}
\label{sec:theory}

Let $\bm e_n$ be the error after $n$ steps. One step maps this error to
$\bm e_{n+1}=\mathsf J_n\bm e_n+\bm\tau_n+\bm\rho_n$, where $\mathsf J_n$ is the
Jacobian of the flow across the step. The step introduces the local truncation
error $\bm\tau_n=O(h^5)$ of RK4 and the rounding error $\bm\rho_n=O(2^{-p})$.
Unrolling the recursion, we write the error at time $t$ as a sum over all
earlier steps of the error introduced there, carried forward by the product of
the intervening Jacobians. In a chaotic system, this product grows on average
as $e^{\lambda(t-t_j)}$, where $\lambda$ is the largest Lyapunov exponent.
Hence, the sum is a geometric series dominated by its earliest terms, and
\begin{equation}
e(t)\approx\left(A\,h^{4}+B\,2^{-p}h^{-\gamma}\right)e^{\lambda t}.
\label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
The first term collects $1/h$ truncation errors of size $h^5$ per unit time. The
second collects $1/h$ rounding errors of size $2^{-p}$ per unit time, which add
like a random walk if their signs are independent, giving $\gamma=\tfrac12$, and
linearly otherwise, giving $\gamma=1$.\cite{hairer2008} Kehlet and
Logg\cite{kehlet2017} derive $\gamma=\tfrac12$. The constants $A$ and
$B$ depend on the trajectory but not on $h$ or $p$.

Setting $e(T)=\epsilon$ for a tolerance $\epsilon$ gives the horizon,
\begin{equation}
T=\frac1\lambda\,\ln\frac{\epsilon}{A\,h^{4}+B\,2^{-p}h^{-\gamma}} .
\label{eq:horizon}
\end{equation}
Everything in the exercise follows from Eq.~(\ref{eq:horizon}). When truncation
dominates and $h=2^{-k}$,
\begin{equation}
T=T_A+\frac{4\ln2}{\lambda}\,k ,
\label{eq:Tk}
\end{equation}
so each halving of the step extends the horizon by $4\ln2/\lambda$, where the
factor of 4 is the order of the method. When roundoff dominates,
\begin{equation}
T=T_B+\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}\left(p-\gamma k\right) ,
\label{eq:Tp}
\end{equation}
so each additional bit extends the horizon by $\ln2/\lambda$, while each halving
of the step now \emph{shortens} the horizon. The regimes meet where the two
terms of Eq.~(\ref{eq:error}) are equal, at
$k^\ast=[\,p+\log_2(A/B)]/(4+\gamma)$, and the horizon there,
\begin{equation}
T_{\max}(p)\approx\mathrm{const}+\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}\,\frac{4p}{4+\gamma},
\label{eq:Tmax}
\end{equation}
is, up to a constant, the longest attainable with $p$ bits and a fourth-order
method. Both slopes contain only $\lambda$ and known constants, so we can
measure $\lambda$ from the numerical error.

as a perturbation of the initial condition grows, since it is itself a
perturbation.

the pendulum passes through more and less unstable parts of its motion.

The smallest precisions fail in a different way, which we now explain. With
$p=11$ and $h=2^{-12}$ the pendulum \emph{never moves}. Wild\cite{wild2019}
observed the same constant solution with eleven bits and $h=10^{-4}$. The cause
is not underflow, since the exponent range of MPFR is never approached. The
velocity $\omega_1$ at first falls by exactly $h$ at every step for 2048 steps.
At $t=\tfrac12$ it reaches $-\tfrac12$, where $h$ is half a unit in the last
place of $\omega_1$, so the sum is a tie, round-to-even returns $-\tfrac12$, and
the velocity is frozen. From then on the increment to the angle is
$h\,|\omega_1|=2^{-13}$, a quarter of the $2^{-11}$ needed to change the last of
eleven bits in $\theta_1=\pi/2$. Hence, the computed pendulum hangs horizontal
forever with a constant velocity that produces no motion. With the same eleven
bits and $h=2^{-8}$ the velocity would not freeze until $|\omega_1|=8$, the
increment $h\,|\omega_1|$ first exceeds $2^{-11}$ at $t=0.129$, and the pendulum
falls.
\ifdefined\longversion
More generally, for this release the velocity freezes at $2^{p-k}$ and the angle
needs $|\omega_1|>2^{k-p}$, so the pendulum stalled for every $h<2^{-p}$ that we
tried and moved for every $h>2^{-p}$. At $h=2^{-p}$ the increment is itself a
tie, so the outcome depends on the last bit of $\pi/2$.
\fi
Here a smaller step has made the error total rather than reducing it, and no
analysis based on small perturbations describes what happens. This stall is an
extreme case of the tendency expressed by the negative sign in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:Tp}).

\subsection{Four measurements of the Lyapunov exponent}

Table~\ref{tab:lambda} collects the estimates. The reference value comes from the
two-trajectory method of Benettin
\textit{et al.}\cite{benettin1976,wolf1985} This method starts a shadow
trajectory at a distance $d_0=10^{-8}$ from the fiducial trajectory, and once per
time unit it accumulates the logarithm of the growth in their separation and
pulls the shadow back to $d_0$. Averaged over $2\times10^4$ time units this gives
$\lambda=\lamBen$ for our initial condition. Of \nEns\ other initial conditions
at rest on the same energy surface, \nChaotic\ are chaotic, with a median of
\lamBenEns, and the remaining \nReg\ lie on regular orbits with $\lambda$
indistinguishable from zero. The direct measurement is the least-squares slope of
$\ln d$ against $t$ for the thin line in Fig.~\ref{fig:step}(b), which is
\lamDir\ over the whole run and \lamDirWin\ for $t>20$, after the slow start
from rest, which is the interval in which every horizon falls.

The two estimates from numerical error agree with each other and with the
directly measured growth of a perturbation over the same interval. All three
exceed the long-time value, by \lamExcessLo\ to \lamExcessHi\%. The difference is
not an error of method, since a horizon samples the growth rate only up to the
horizon, and over its first 160 time units this trajectory happens to be more
unstable than on average. The exponent proper is the limit of long times, and the
finite-time values scatter about this limit. The standard errors quoted above do
not include this scatter. The value from precision varies from \lamPrecCoarse\ to
\lamPrec\ with the step, and the value from the step varies from
\lamStepThrLo\ to \lamStepThrHi\ with the threshold that defines the horizon.
Both variations reflect the fluctuating local growth rate rather than noise.
This distinction matters for students, who otherwise picture exponential
divergence as uniform. We regard the agreement as the central result of the
exercise, because the numerical error that students are taught to treat as a
defect of the computation measures the Lyapunov exponent of the physical system.

In double precision the horizon is about \TBestDouble\ time units, or
\TBestDoubleSec~s for a pendulum with one-meter rods, and is reached at
$h=2^{-\kBestDouble}$. A student who responds to a doubtful result by reducing

the same turnover in the trajectory itself.

\subsection{Energy}
\label{sec:energy}

The error in the energy grows slowly, through the secular
drift of a non-symplectic method, and at $t=160$ is still below $10^{-\dEexp}$
in units of $mgl$. Meanwhile, the error in the position passes $10^{-2}l$ at
$t\approx\TdoubleKten$ and grows to the length of a rod by
$t\approx\TdoubleRod$. Energy constrains the state to a three-dimensional
surface in the four-dimensional phase space, and the divergence of nearby
trajectories takes place within that surface. A computed state can drift an
arbitrary distance without leaving this surface.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What accuracy costs}

The work of a fixed-step integration is proportional to $2^{k}$. By
Eq.~(\ref{eq:Tk}), extending the horizon by $\Delta T$ requires
$\Delta k=\lambda\,\Delta T/(4\ln2)$, so the work is multiplied by
$e^{\lambda\Delta T/4}$. To stay on the optimum of Eq.~(\ref{eq:Tmax}), the
precision must also rise by about $\lambda\,\Delta T\,(4+\gamma)/(4\ln2)$ bits.
Suppose we want to double the double-precision horizon. This means
extrapolating beyond the times sampled, so we use the long-time exponent
\lamBen, and then the number of steps is multiplied by roughly
$e^{\lambda T_{\max}/4}\approx\costFactor$ and about \extraBits\ additional bits
are needed. At the finite-time rate
\lamStep\ measured over the first 160 time units, the figures would be nearer
\costFactorFT\ and \extraBitsFT, but either way the horizon grows as the
logarithm of the effort. A method of order $n$ replaces the 4 by $n$, which is
the main argument for high-order integrators in this setting. We checked this
at low order, where at a fixed early time the error of forward Euler scales as
$h^{\ordEuler}$, that of the explicit midpoint rule as $h^{\ordMid}$, and that
of RK4 as $h^{\rkOrder}$. Forward Euler with $h=2^{-16}$ is accurate for only
\TEulerFine\ time units, which is less than the \TRKcoarse\ that RK4 achieves
with $h=2^{-4}$ and a thousandth of the work. Furthermore, all of the forward
Euler horizons fall inside the slow start of the motion, so its slope against
$k$ does not give $\lambda$.

In everyday units, the long-time exponent for one-meter rods is
$\lambda=\lamSI~\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so reducing the step tenfold adds
$4\ln10/\lambda\approx\decadeSI$~s of accurate motion, or \decadeSIFT~s at the
finite-time rate, at ten times the cost.

\subsection{What is still right after the horizon}

Beyond $T$, the computed trajectory is not the solution of the initial-value
problem posed. However, this trajectory is still meaningful, since shadowing
results\cite{hammel1987,hammel1988,grebogi1990,sauer1997,hayes2004} show that a
noisy computed orbit of a chaotic system typically stays close to \emph{some}
exact orbit with a slightly different initial condition for times much longer
than the horizon. Hence, we expect that properties of the ensemble of orbits
rather than of a particular orbit, such as the Lyapunov exponent, are computed
correctly by runs hundreds of horizons long. The first row of
Table~\ref{tab:lambda} is such a computation in double precision. We suggest
ending a class with this distinction between predicting a trajectory and
sampling the dynamics.

\section{Suggested problems}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Order of the method.} Replace RK4 with the explicit midpoint rule,
repeat the first experiment, and compare the slope of $T$ against $k$ with
$2\ln2/\lambda$. Then try forward Euler and explain why its slope does not give
$\lambda$. Estimate the step that Euler's method would need to match RK4 at
$h=2^{-10}$.
\item \emph{Energy dependence.} Release the pendulum from rest at smaller angles
and show that $\lambda$ decreases and the horizon lengthens. For sufficiently
small angles, show that the error grows as a power of $t$ rather than
exponentially, and determine what Eq.~(\ref{eq:horizon}) becomes.
\item \emph{The roundoff exponent.} Repeat the second experiment at several
steps and determine $\gamma$. Then, replace the update
$\bm y\leftarrow\bm y+\Delta\bm y$ by compensated summation\cite{higham2002} and
determine $\gamma$ again.
\ifdefined\longversion
\item \emph{A symplectic method.} Integrate with the implicit midpoint rule,
which is symplectic, and show that the energy error no longer drifts. Compare
the horizon with that of the explicit midpoint rule at the same step. Explain
whether preserving the geometry of phase space helps predict the trajectory.
\item \emph{Another system.} Repeat the first and third experiments for the
Lorenz equations with the standard parameters, for which
$\lambda\approx0.906$, and predict the double-precision horizon before it is
measured.
\fi
\item \emph{Shadowing.} Compute the time average of the kinetic energy over
$10^4$ time units in single and double precision with $h=2^{-7}$. We find
1.357 and 1.358 in units of $mgl$, but the trajectories separate after fifty
time units. Explain why the two averages agree to a part in a thousand.
\end{enumerate}
