\begin{abstract}
\noindent We report a computational project, begun in an undergraduate course in
numerical analysis, that asks for how long a computed trajectory is accurate.
Students who simulate chaotic systems rarely ask this question about their own
programs. We integrate the double pendulum with a fixed-step fourth-order
Runge--Kutta method in arbitrary-precision arithmetic, so we can vary the step
$h$ and the number of significand bits $p$ independently and measure the effects
of truncation and of roundoff separately. The time $T$ for which a run agrees
with a far more accurate reference obeys a single law governed by the largest
Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$. Each halving of $h$ extends $T$ by $4\ln2/\lambda$
and each additional bit extends this time by $\ln2/\lambda$. For any given
precision there is a best step beyond which refinement shortens $T$. The slopes
yield $\lambda=\lamStep\pm\lamStepSE$ and $\lamPrec\pm\lamPrecSE$, which are
equal to the growth rate of a perturbed trajectory over the same interval and
within 20\% of the long-time exponent \lamBen\ from the standard two-trajectory
method. In double precision the pendulum cannot be followed for more than about
\TBestDouble\ natural time units with this method, but its energy stays constant
to $10^{-\dEexp}$ in units of $mgl$ throughout. The project is suitable for advanced
undergraduates in physics or applied mathematics and requires only a short
program. It shows that numerical error is a perturbation like any other, and
that its growth measures the dynamics.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} double pendulum, chaos, Lyapunov exponent, numerical
integration, roundoff error, computational physics education

\section{Introduction}

The double pendulum is among the simplest mechanical systems that are chaotic,
and it is correspondingly popular in teaching. In this journal,
D'Alessio~\cite{dalessio2023} has given a combined analytical, numerical, and
experimental treatment, including the full spectrum of Lyapunov exponents, aimed
at upper-year undergraduates. Earlier pedagogical studies measured the
divergence of two real pendulums released together~\cite{shinbrot1992},
developed the apparatus as a laboratory experiment~\cite{levien1993}, and mapped
the onset of chaos numerically as a function of energy~\cite{stachowiak2006}.
Calv\~ao and Penna~\cite{calvao2015} reviewed in this journal the numerical
methods used to study the system, and a recent pedagogical review is given in
reference~\cite{oliveira2024}. In
all of them, the simulation is a tool, and its output is taken to be the motion
of the pendulum, but this paper studies the tool.

Sensitive dependence on initial conditions means that a small perturbation grows
exponentially, and a numerical integrator supplies a small perturbation at every
step, by truncating the Taylor series of the solution and by rounding each
arithmetic result. Hence, the computed trajectory must leave the true trajectory
after a finite time, however carefully the program is written.
Lorenz~\cite{lorenz1963} met chaos in this way, and
Lighthill~\cite{lighthill1986} later called the time in question the horizon of
predictability. The reliability of long computations of chaotic systems has
since been examined in atmospheric modelling~\cite{teixeira2007} and with
multiple-precision arithmetic~\cite{liao2009}. In particular, Li
\textit{et al}~\cite{li2001} showed that a given precision has a best step and a
longest reliable time, and Kehlet and Logg~\cite{kehlet2017} derived an error
estimate of the form we use below. Wang and Li~\cite{wang2014} found that the
reliable time is linear in the number of digits, with a slope set by the
Lyapunov exponent, and Mendes and Nepomuceno~\cite{mendes2016} estimated the
exponent from the divergence of two equivalent computations that round
differently. These studies treat the Lorenz and similar systems and are written
for researchers. For students, Hayes~\cite{hayes2004} discussed what a computed
chaotic trajectory means, and Faux and Godolphin~\cite{faux2021} collected
floating-point pitfalls. In an undergraduate thesis, Wild~\cite{wild2019}
integrated the double pendulum with RK4 at the IEEE significand widths and
observed that the wider formats follow a 113-bit solution for longer, but did not
relate this time to the Lyapunov exponent. However, in our experience,
students who simulate a double pendulum are seldom asked how long their result
can be believed, and the informal checks they apply, such as watching the
energy, cannot answer this question.

This work began as our final project in an undergraduate course on numerical
analysis. We simulated identical double pendulums with different steps and
different floating-point formats and watched the trajectories separate. The
observation that prompted this paper was that a step ten times smaller, at ten
times the cost, added only five to ten seconds of agreement. Here we redo the
project with a cleaner design, explain that observation, and show that the
numerical error measures the Lyapunov exponent, so the explanation is
quantitative.

We believe the project is useful to physics education at the advanced
undergraduate level in three ways. First, it gives students a concrete meaning
for the Lyapunov exponent in terms of something they control. Second, it
separates truncation error from roundoff error, which are usually taught
together and rarely distinguished in practice. Finally, it corrects two common
beliefs, that a smaller step always gives a better answer and that conservation
of energy certifies a trajectory.

\section{The project}

\subsection{Model}

Two point masses $m$ are suspended in series from a fixed pivot by massless rigid
rods of length $l$ and move in a vertical plane. The angles $\theta_1$ and
$\theta_2$ are measured from the downward vertical. Measuring time in units of
$\sqrt{l/g}$ (which is \secPerUnit~s for $l=1$~m) removes every parameter from
the Lagrangian equations of motion, which become
\begin{align}
\ddot\theta_1 &= \frac{\omega_1^2\sin\Delta\cos\Delta+\sin\theta_2\cos\Delta
                +\omega_2^2\sin\Delta-2\sin\theta_1}{2-\cos^2\Delta},\label{eq:eom1}\\
\ddot\theta_2 &= \frac{-\omega_2^2\sin\Delta\cos\Delta
                +2\left(\sin\theta_1\cos\Delta-\omega_1^2\sin\Delta-\sin\theta_2\right)}
                {2-\cos^2\Delta},\label{eq:eom2}
\end{align}
with $\omega_i=\dot\theta_i$ and $\Delta=\theta_2-\theta_1$. The conserved energy
in units of $mgl$ is
\begin{equation}
E=\omega_1^2+\tfrac12\omega_2^2+\omega_1\omega_2\cos\Delta-2\cos\theta_1-\cos\theta_2 .
\end{equation}
The pendulum is released from rest with both rods horizontal, so $E=0$.

\subsection{Numerical method}

Equations (\ref{eq:eom1}) and (\ref{eq:eom2}) are written as a first-order system
for $\bm y=(\theta_1,\theta_2,\omega_1,\omega_2)$ and advanced with the classical
fourth-order Runge--Kutta method (RK4)~\cite{hairer1993} at a fixed step $h$. We
avoid adaptive step-size control deliberately, since its purpose is to hide the
dependence on $h$ that we want to see.

The arithmetic is done by the MPFR library~\cite{fousse2007}, which rounds each
operation correctly to a significand of $p$ bits, where $p$ is set by the
program. Our first version used Julia's \texttt{BigFloat} type and the present
version uses Python with \texttt{gmpy2}, and both are thin wrappers around MPFR.
With $p=24$, 53, 64, and 113 the significand has the width of the IEEE single,
double, extended, and quadruple formats~\cite{ieee754,goldberg1991}. With $p=237$, the
width of the 256-bit format, the rounding unit is $10^{-71}$, so the arithmetic
is exact for our purposes.

The choice of step proved important, since our first version used decimal steps
such as $h=10^{-4}$ and no binary format represents $10^{-4}$ exactly. Hence,
runs at different precisions were also using slightly different steps. We now
take $h=2^{-k}$, which is exact at every precision, so changing $p$ changes only
the arithmetic. Wild~\cite{wild2019} observed the effect of this choice when
replacing $h=10^{-4}$ by $h=2^{-13}$ kept the 53-bit and 64-bit solutions close
to the 113-bit solution for longer.

\subsection{Measuring error without an exact solution}

We measure the error of a run by the distance $d(t)$, in units of $l$, between
its lower bob and that of a reference run. Because both bobs lie within $2l$ of
the pivot, $d$ cannot exceed 4, and its running maximum levels off near that
bound when two runs have become unrelated. The
\emph{horizon} $T$ of a run is the first time at which $d\ge10^{-2}$, which is
about when two superimposed animations can be told apart by eye.

The reference is a much more accurate run, and an elementary argument says how
far the reference can be trusted. If $e_a$ and $e_r$ are the true errors of a
run and of the reference, the triangle inequality gives $|d-e_a|\le e_r$. Both
errors are amplified by the same dynamics, so their ratio stays near its initial
value, which for RK4 runs with steps $2^{-k_a}$ and $2^{-k_r}$ is
$2^{-4(k_r-k_a)}$. With $k_r=18$, the separation $d$ equals the true error of
every run with $k_a\le16$ to better than half a per cent, until that run reaches
its own horizon.

\subsection{Three experiments}

\begin{description}
\item[Truncation only.] With $p=237$, roundoff is fifty orders of magnitude
below truncation, so we vary $k$ from 4 to 17 and compare with $k=18$.
\item[Roundoff only.] With $h$ fixed, we vary $p$ from 11 to 128 and compare
with the run at $p=237$ and the \emph{same} $h$. The two runs iterate the same
discrete map, so their truncation errors are identical and cancel, which leaves
only rounding. Ordinary hardware arithmetic offers no way of making this
separation.
\item[Both.] With $p=24$, 32, 40, 53, and 64, we vary $k$ and compare with the
reference of the first experiment. This is the position of a student using
single or double precision.
\end{description}

\section{A model for the horizon}
\label{sec:model}

Let $\bm e_n$ be the error after $n$ steps, and let $\mathsf J_n$ be the
Jacobian of the flow over the next step. Then,
$\bm e_{n+1}=\mathsf J_n\bm e_n+\bm\tau_n+\bm\rho_n$, where $\bm\tau_n=O(h^5)$
is the local truncation error and $\bm\rho_n=O(2^{-p})$ is the rounding error.
Thus, the error at time $t$ is a sum of the errors introduced at earlier steps,
each propagated by the product of the Jacobians in between. For a chaotic
trajectory, that product grows on average as $e^{\lambda(t-t_j)}$, where
$\lambda$ is the largest Lyapunov exponent, so the sum is dominated by its
earliest terms:
\begin{equation}
e(t)\approx\left(A\,h^{4}+B\,2^{-p}h^{-\gamma}\right)e^{\lambda t}.
\label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
The first term comes from $1/h$ truncation errors of size $h^5$ per unit time. The
second comes from $1/h$ rounding errors of size $2^{-p}$ per unit time, where
$\gamma=\tfrac12$ if they accumulate as a random walk and $\gamma=1$ if they
accumulate systematically~\cite{hairer2008}. Kehlet and
Logg~\cite{kehlet2017} derive $\gamma=\tfrac12$. Setting $e(T)=\epsilon$ gives
\begin{equation}
T=\frac1\lambda\ln\frac{\epsilon}{A\,h^{4}+B\,2^{-p}h^{-\gamma}} .
\label{eq:horizon}
\end{equation}
With $h=2^{-k}$ the two limiting cases are
\begin{align}
T&=T_A+\frac{4\ln2}{\lambda}\,k &&\text{(truncation dominant)},\label{eq:Tk}\\
T&=T_B+\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}\,(p-\gamma k) &&\text{(roundoff dominant)}.\label{eq:Tp}
\end{align}
Halving the step extends the horizon by a fixed amount when truncation dominates
and shortens it when roundoff dominates. Meanwhile, each extra bit extends the
horizon by a fixed amount. The regimes meet at
$k^\ast=[p+\log_2(A/B)]/(4+\gamma)$, where the horizon takes its greatest value
for that precision,
$T_{\max}\approx\mathrm{const}+(\ln2/\lambda)\,4p/(4+\gamma)$. Note that these
slopes depend on the pendulum only through $\lambda$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Step size}

Figure~\ref{fig:snap} shows three pendulums that differ only in step. They are
indistinguishable for tens of time units and then separate one at a time, largest
step first. Figure~\ref{fig:step}(a) shows the corresponding errors, which rise
at a common average rate over many orders of magnitude. They are evenly spaced
because they are the same curve scaled by $h^4$, as figure~\ref{fig:step}(b)
shows. Multiplied by $h^{-4}$, they coincide to within \collapse\ decades for
every $h\le2^{-8}$ wherever none has saturated, and the error at $t=10$ scales
as $h^{\rkOrder}$.

The common curve is the amplification factor of the trajectory. To check this,
we perturbed $\theta_1(0)$ by $2^{-170}$, which is small enough to stay in the
linear regime for the whole run. The resulting separation, drawn as the thin
line in figure~\ref{fig:step}(b), follows the collapsed error curves through
every plateau and burst. The integrator's error grows as a perturbation of the
initial condition grows because the error is itself a perturbation. The growth
is exponential only on average, since the local rate varies as the pendulum
visits more and less unstable configurations.

Figure~\ref{fig:hor}(a) shows the horizon against $k$. A line fitted over
$7\le k\le16$ has slope $\slopeStep\pm\slopeStepSE$ time units per halving, so
equation~(\ref{eq:Tk}) gives $\lambda=\lamStep\pm\lamStepSE$. The scatter about
the line is real, since a horizon that falls just before a burst of growth gains
little from the next halving and a horizon that falls just after gains a great
deal. The grey staircase in figure~\ref{fig:hor}(a) is the horizon predicted for
every $h$ from the measured growth of the $2^{-170}$ perturbation, where the one
constant $A$ of equation~(\ref{eq:error}) is fixed by the collapse. The
staircase tracks the measured horizons to within a few time units, and its
risers fall where the horizons jump.

\subsection{Precision}

Figure~\ref{fig:prec} shows the second experiment at $h=2^{-12}$, where the
pattern is that of figure~\ref{fig:step} with $2^{-p}$ in place of $h^4$. The
horizons in figure~\ref{fig:hor}(b) have slope $\slopePrec\pm\slopePrecSE$ time
units per bit, so equation~(\ref{eq:Tp}) gives $\lambda=\lamPrec\pm\lamPrecSE$.
The fit uses $24\le p\le80$, since below 24 the horizons fall inside the slow
start from rest and above 80 they lie beyond the 160 time units over which the
growth of the perturbation was measured.
Changing the step by a factor of 256 shifts the line only slightly. Comparing
errors at fixed early times for $h=2^{-8}$ and $2^{-16}$ gives
$\gamma\approx\gammaRound$, close to the random-walk value of $\tfrac12$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{fig3_precision_sweep}
\caption{Separation from the 237-bit run at the same step, $h=2^{-12}$, for eight
significand widths. Truncation error is common to each pair and cancels, so

We left the lowest precisions out of the fit because with $p=11$ and
$h=2^{-12}$ the pendulum never moves (the result we remember best from the
project). Wild~\cite{wild2019} observed the same constant solution with eleven
bits and $h=10^{-4}$. The cause is not underflow, since the exponent range of
MPFR is never approached. At first the velocity $\omega_1$ falls by exactly $h$ per step for
2048 steps. At $t=\tfrac12$ it reaches $-\tfrac12$, where round-to-even freezes
the velocity because $h$ is half a unit in the last place of $\omega_1$. The
increment to the angle is then $h\,|\omega_1|=2^{-13}$ for ever, a quarter of
the $2^{-11}$ needed to change the last of eleven bits in $\theta_1=\pi/2$.
Hence, the pendulum hangs horizontally with a constant velocity that produces no
motion. With the same eleven bits and $h=2^{-8}$ the increment first exceeds
$2^{-11}$ at $t=0.129$ and the pendulum falls, so reducing the step has made the
error total. Since the velocity freezes at $2^{p-k}$ and the angle needs
$|\omega_1|>2^{k-p}$, the pattern is general for this release, and the pendulum
stalled for every $h<2^{-p}$ we tried and moved for every $h>2^{-p}$. At
$h=2^{-p}$ the increment is itself a tie, so the outcome depends on the last bit
of $\pi/2$.

\subsection{The Lyapunov exponent four ways}

Table~\ref{tab:lambda} compares the estimates. The conventional value is from
the two-trajectory method of Benettin
\textit{et al}~\cite{benettin1976,wolf1985}. A shadow orbit at
distance $10^{-8}$ is advanced with the fiducial orbit, and once per time unit
the logarithm of the growth of their separation is accumulated and the shadow is
returned to its original distance. Over $2\times10^4$ time units this gives
$\lambda=\lamBen$. Of \nEns\ other states at rest with the same energy,
\nChaotic\ are chaotic with median \lamBenEns, and \nReg\ lie on regular orbits.
The least-squares slope of $\ln d$ against $t$ for the $2^{-170}$ perturbation
is \lamDir\ over the whole run and \lamDirWin\ for $t>20$, after the slow start
from rest, which is the interval in which every horizon falls.

The two values obtained from numerical error agree with each other and with the
direct growth rate over the same interval. All three exceed the long-time value,
by \lamExcessLo\ to \lamExcessHi\%. This is not a failure of the method, since a horizon
samples the growth rate only up to the horizon, and over its first 160 time
units this trajectory is more unstable than on average. The exponent proper is a
long-time limit, and finite-time values scatter about this limit. The standard
errors quoted do not include this scatter, since the value from precision varies
from \lamPrecCoarse\ to \lamPrec\ with the step and the value from the step
varies from \lamStepThrLo\ to \lamStepThrHi\ with the threshold that defines the
horizon. Students who
picture exponential divergence as uniform do well to learn this distinction.

\subsection{Working precision}

Figure~\ref{fig:turn} shows the third experiment, where all precisions agree at
large steps, since truncation dominates. As the step is reduced, each curve
leaves the 237-bit line in turn and flattens, and at the lower precisions it
then falls, as equation~(\ref{eq:horizon}) predicts. Table~\ref{tab:best} lists
the best step and longest horizon for each precision. In double precision the
longest horizon is \TBestDouble\ time units (\TBestDoubleSec~s for one-metre
rods), reached at $h=2^{-\kBestDouble}$. Reducing the step further to $2^{-17}$
costs \kRatio\ times as much and gives \TdoubleFine. In single precision the best
horizon is \TBestSingle, at $h=2^{-\kBestSingle}$, and by $h=2^{-17}$ it has
fallen to \TsingleFine. Calv\~ao and Penna~\cite{calvao2015}, judging RK4 on this
system by its energy error, likewise found an optimum near a step of $10^{-4}$
with smaller steps giving less precision, and the horizon $T$ shows the same
turnover in the trajectory itself.

\subsection{Energy}

Figure~\ref{fig:energy} shows both errors for an ordinary double-precision run
with $h=2^{-10}$. The energy error drifts slowly upward, as expected of a
non-symplectic method, and never exceeds $10^{-\dEexp}$ in units of $mgl$, but the position error
passes $10^{-2}l$ at $t\approx\TdoubleKten$ and grows to the length of a rod by
$t\approx\TdoubleRod$. Energy confines the state to a three-dimensional
surface in phase space, and chaotic divergence takes place within that surface,
so a computed state can wander arbitrarily far without leaving the surface.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The price of a longer horizon}

The work of a fixed-step run is proportional to $2^k$. From
equation~(\ref{eq:Tk}), a gain of $\Delta T$ needs
$\Delta k=\lambda\Delta T/(4\ln2)$, which multiplies the work by
$e^{\lambda\Delta T/4}$, and the precision must rise by about
$\lambda\Delta T(4+\gamma)/(4\ln2)$ bits to stay at the optimum. Doubling the
double-precision horizon of this pendulum means extrapolating beyond the times
sampled, so we use the long-time exponent \lamBen. The number of steps is
multiplied by about \costFactor\ and some \extraBits\ more bits are needed. At
the finite-time rate \lamStep\ of the first 160 time units the figures would be
nearer \costFactorFT\ and \extraBitsFT, but either way the horizon grows as the
logarithm of the effort. In everyday units the long-time exponent is
$\lambda=\lamSI$~s$^{-1}$ for one-metre rods, so reducing the step tenfold adds
$4\ln10/\lambda\approx\decadeSI$~s (\decadeSIFT~s at the finite-time rate), as
we saw in our first crude runs.

A method of order $n$ replaces 4 by $n$, and we verified that the error of
forward Euler at fixed time scales as $h^{\ordEuler}$ and that of the explicit
midpoint rule as $h^{\ordMid}$. Forward Euler with $h=2^{-16}$ has a horizon of
only \TEulerFine\ time units, shorter than the \TRKcoarse\ of RK4 with
$h=2^{-4}$, and its horizons all fall inside the slow start of the motion, so
its slope against $k$ does not give $\lambda$. For chaotic problems the case for
high-order methods is stronger than usual.

\subsection{What survives the horizon}

After $T$ the computed trajectory is no longer the solution of the problem
posed. However, shadowing
results~\cite{hammel1987,hammel1988,grebogi1990,sauer1997,hayes2004} show that it typically
stays close to an exact trajectory with slightly different initial data for
times much longer than the horizon. This is the reason to expect that averages
over the motion are still computed correctly, and the
first row of table~\ref{tab:lambda} relies on this fact. As a check, we
computed the time-averaged kinetic energy over $10^4$ time units in single and
double precision. The two trajectories part after about fifty time units, but
the averages are 1.357 and 1.358.

\subsection{Running the project with a class}

The program is the usual RK4 with the number type changed, and apart from the
two finest reference runs everything reported here finishes in minutes on a
laptop. In the natural sequence we followed, students first overlay animations
of runs that differ in step or precision and see them separate. They then plot
the separation on a logarithmic scale and find straight lines, fit the
horizons, and compare the resulting $\lambda$ with a two-trajectory estimate.
Students without a multiple-precision library can still carry out the third
experiment, using double precision at the best step of table~\ref{tab:best} as
the reference for single precision. A natural extension is other energies,
where $\lambda$ is smaller and some orbits are regular, so the error grows only
as a power of $t$. Another is a symplectic integrator, to find out whether
preserving phase-space structure helps with prediction. A third is other
chaotic systems, such as the Lorenz equations, for which the double-precision
horizon can be predicted from the known exponent before it is measured.

\section{Conclusion}

A simulation of a chaotic system is accurate for a time that depends
logarithmically on the error introduced per step. For the double pendulum
integrated by RK4, that time increases by $4\ln2/\lambda$ per halving of the
step and by $\ln2/\lambda$ per bit of precision, up to a ceiling set by
whichever source of error is larger. In double precision, the ceiling is about
\TBestDouble\ natural time units. The same measurements give the finite-time
Lyapunov exponent of the trajectory to within a few per cent, and its long-time
value to within twenty per cent. Students who complete the project see that the
system responds to truncation and roundoff as it responds to any other
perturbation, and they learn to ask for how long any simulation is accurate.
